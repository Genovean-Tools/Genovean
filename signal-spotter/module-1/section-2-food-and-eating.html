Signal Spotter: Food and Eating

Nutrition often declines quietly and early. This section helps you look honestly at what is -- and is not -- happening around food in your parent's daily life.

Food and Eating
1 The fridge, pantry, or cupboards are not being stocked properly Not reviewed

You open the fridge and there is very little in it. The same few basics that were there last week are still there, barely touched, or there is almost nothing at all. The bread has gone stale. The fruit has not been replaced. The staples they always kept -- whatever those were for them -- are missing. Shopping that used to happen routinely has either stopped or become much less frequent. This can happen because getting to a shop has become harder, because appetite has reduced, because the cognitive organisation required for regular shopping has become more difficult, or simply because the motivation to eat well has quietly faded.

What to look for

Open the fridge when you visit. Look at what is there and what is not. Think about what they used to always have. Is the milk fresh? Are there vegetables or just condiments? Look at the bread bin, the fruit bowl, the freezer. Notice whether the food there looks like it is being eaten or whether it has been sitting.

How to observe without making it an issue

Offering to do a shop together or bringing a few things when you visit is a natural way to look at what they have without making it an inspection. Asking "what have you been eating lately?" in passing often gives you more information than looking in the fridge. The answer -- or the vagueness of the answer -- tells you a great deal.

Example of a useful note

"Visited on a Sunday. Fridge had milk, a bit of cheese, and some leftovers that looked a few days old. No fresh vegetables. The bread on the counter was stale. She said she had not gotten around to shopping this week. When I asked what she had been eating she said mostly toast and soup from a tin. This used to be someone who cooked a proper meal every evening."

Why this note matters. A fridge that is not being stocked is one of the most reliable early indicators that daily functioning has shifted. Writing down specifically what you saw -- and when -- gives you a before and after that is difficult to argue with later.
2 Cooking has become very simple, very repetitive, or has mostly stopped Not reviewed

Someone who used to cook proper meals is now eating the same two or three simple things on rotation. Toast. Tinned soup. A bowl of cereal. Reheated leftovers that have been in the fridge for days. Or cooking has stopped almost entirely and meals are being replaced by snacks or takeaway. Preparing a meal requires planning, physical energy, the ability to manage multiple steps at once, and the motivation to eat well. When any of those things start to slip, the food gets simpler. It is one of the clearest early signals, and one of the easiest to explain away as a preference rather than a change.

What to look for

Ask what they had for dinner yesterday, and the day before. Notice whether the answer is always the same type of thing. If you visit at mealtimes, observe what is being prepared -- or whether anything is being prepared at all. Look at the pots, pans, and oven. Signs that they are not being used regularly are easy to spot once you are looking for them.

How to observe without making it an issue

Sharing a meal is one of the most natural ways to observe. Cook something together when you visit, or bring a meal and eat with them. This gives you direct sight of how they are eating without making it feel like a welfare check. Notice how much they eat, whether they seem interested in the food, and whether they mention what they usually have at home.

Example of a useful note

"Asked what he had eaten over the weekend. Saturday was toast for breakfast and a tin of beans for dinner. Sunday was cereal twice. He used to make a roast on Sundays without fail. The oven looked like it had not been used in weeks. He brushed it off and said he had not been that hungry."

Why this note matters. The shift from proper cooking to simple or no cooking often happens so gradually that it becomes normal before anyone names it. Writing down what you observed -- specifically and with a date -- is how you establish that this is a change, not a preference.
3 Noticeable weight loss or a change in physical appearance around eating Not reviewed

Clothes that used to fit are now hanging loose. A face that looks thinner than you remember. A general sense that they look lighter, less substantial, less themselves. Weight loss in older adults can happen quickly when nutrition declines, and the change can be hard to see when you are visiting regularly because it happens gradually. If you have not seen them for a few months and the change is visible, that is significant. If you see them often and have a quiet feeling that something has changed, trust that feeling and look for the evidence of it.

What to look for

Notice how their clothes fit. Look at their face and arms. Ask someone who sees them less frequently whether they have noticed any changes. Compare a recent photo to one from a year ago if you can. You are not trying to diagnose anything -- you are simply trying to establish whether their physical condition has changed and whether food is likely to be a factor.

How to observe without making it an issue

Do not comment on weight or appearance directly unless you need to. If you are genuinely concerned, a gentle opening is to ask how their appetite has been lately, whether they have been enjoying their food, or whether anything has been putting them off eating. Their answer will often tell you more than you expect.

4 Meals being skipped or replaced with very small amounts of food Not reviewed

Breakfast is a cup of tea and nothing else. Lunch is forgotten or not bothered with. Dinner is a few biscuits or whatever was easiest to reach. The structure of eating -- three meals, or at least a consistent pattern -- has broken down. This can happen because appetite has genuinely declined, because depression or low mood has reduced interest in food, because the effort of preparing something feels like too much, or because they are forgetting to eat. Any of these is worth taking seriously. Consistently low food intake has real consequences for energy, cognition, and physical health.

What to look for

Ask specifically about meals, not just food in general. "What did you have for breakfast this morning?" or "Did you have lunch today?" gives you more useful information than "Are you eating all right?" Look at the plates in the drying rack, the pots that have been used, the wrappers in the bin. These things tell you more than the conversation does.

How to observe without making it an issue

If you can, visit at a mealtime occasionally. Suggesting you bring lunch or cook dinner together on a visit gives you direct sight of how they are eating without it feeling like an inspection. It is also simply a kind thing to do, which means they are more likely to let you in without becoming defensive about what you might see.

Example of a useful note

"Called at lunchtime on a Wednesday. She had not eaten yet and said she had not been hungry. When I visited on Friday she had had a cup of tea and a couple of biscuits by mid-afternoon and called that her lunch. Why this note matters. Skipping meals regularly is not a habit to monitor and wait on. It is a signal that needs to be part of the wider picture you are building. The specific detail you record here -- which meals, how often, what they said -- is the kind of information that makes a difference in a conversation with a doctor or with other family members.
