Early Signs: Getting Around

Independence and movement are deeply tied to wellbeing. Changes in how your parent gets around often appear before anything else does. This section asks you to look honestly at what you have already noticed.

Getting Around
1 Driving has become a concern, near misses, new damage, or avoidance Not reviewed

New scrapes or dents on the car that were not there before. A story about a close call on a road they have driven for years. Refusing to drive at night, on motorways, or in conditions they used to handle without hesitation. Driving much less than they used to without being able to say clearly why. These are not small things. For many older adults, driving is independence. It is how they get to appointments, see friends, manage errands, and maintain a sense of being in control of their own life. When driving starts to change, the implications go far beyond the car. This sign is one of the most emotionally charged in the entire toolkit, and one of the most important to document carefully before any conversation about it happens.

What to look for

Walk past the car when you visit and take a casual look. Notice new marks that were not there before. If you travel with them, pay attention to how they drive ,hesitation at junctions, difficulty judging distances, slower reactions, getting confused on familiar routes. Ask whether they have been getting out much lately. A parent who used to be constantly on the go becoming housebound is itself a signal that driving may have quietly become something they are avoiding.

How to observe without making it an issue

Do not raise this today. This section is about observing and recording, not having the conversation. The driving conversation is one of the most sensitive in family caregiving and needs to be timed and framed carefully. For now your job is to write down what you have seen or felt -- the specific details, the dates, the incidents, so that when the time comes to raise it, you are doing so from a place of clear, documented observation rather than a feeling you cannot quite articulate.

Example of a useful note

"Noticed in February there was a new scrape along the passenger side of the car that he could not explain. He got confused on the way back from his sister's in March, a route he has driven every week for twenty years. He brushed it off as a one-off. He has not driven at night since November and he used to go out regularly in the evenings."

Why this note matters. Driving concerns that are vaguely felt but not documented are almost impossible to act on. Specific incidents with dates are not. What you write here is what makes the eventual conversation possible -- and what makes it fair to everyone in it.
2 Getting lost or confused in familiar places or on familiar routes Not reviewed

Getting turned around in a town they have lived in for decades. Difficulty navigating a route they have taken hundreds of times. Arriving somewhere later than expected and being unable to say clearly what happened. Mentioning that they went the wrong way or ended up somewhere they did not intend. This kind of spatial confusion is different from occasionally missing a turn. It is a signal that spatial memory and navigation, both of which rely on cognitive processes that can be affected early in decline, may be changing. It is easy to miss because parents will often cover for it, avoid the routes that have become difficult, or simply not mention it.

What to look for

Ask casually where they have been and how the journey was. Notice if their answer is vague or if they seem slightly uneasy about it. Pay attention if they mention a detour, getting stuck, or arriving later than expected. If you travel with them, watch whether they navigate with the same confidence they used to have or whether they seem less certain. Reduced confidence on familiar roads is the signal.

How to observe without making it an issue

Ask about journeys in the same tone you would ask about anything else. "How was the drive?" is not an interrogation. If they mention something that concerns you, note it down after the conversation rather than pressing for more detail in the moment. Your job right now is to build a picture, not to test them. The fact that they mentioned it at all is already important information.

Example of a useful note

"She mentioned last month that she had ended up on the wrong road on the way back from the supermarket and had to ask someone for directions. She has shopped at that supermarket for fifteen years. She laughed it off. Last week she arrived at my house forty minutes later than expected and said she had taken a wrong turn. She seemed a bit shaken."

Why this note matters. Confusion on familiar routes is one of the earlier cognitive signals to appear and one of the easier ones to dismiss. Writing down the specific instances, where, when, what they said me,ans you have something real and dated rather than a vague impression.
3 Going out much less, activities, visits, and errands being quietly dropped Not reviewed

They used to go to a club, a class, a regular lunch, a weekly shop. They used to visit a friend or a sibling. Now they are staying home more and the reasons are always slightly different, not feeling great, the weather, it is not worth the bother. Individual reasons are easy to accept. A pattern of reasons is a different thing. When a person who was active and social gradually stops going out, it is rarely because nothing particular happened. It is usually because something -- transport difficulty, physical confidence, cognitive anxiety about unfamiliar situations, or simply a loss of energy and motivation -- has made going out feel harder than it is worth. The activities get dropped quietly, one at a time, until staying home has become the default.

What to look for

Think about what they used to do regularly six months or a year ago that they are no longer doing now. Ask about specific things by name, the club, the friend, the weekly outing. Notice whether the answer is a specific reason or a vague one. Notice whether they seem to miss it or whether they seem to have let it go without much feeling about it at all. Both are worth recording.

How to observe without making it an issue

Ask about people and places rather than habits. "How is Margaret? Have you seen her lately?" is easier to answer honestly than "Are you still going out as much as you used to?" The first question opens a conversation. The second puts them on the defensive. What you learn from the first answer -- and how they answer it is, usually enough to tell you what you need to know.

Example of a useful note

"He stopped going to his Thursday bowling club about three months ago. Said his knee was bothering him, but he has not mentioned the knee since. He used to visit his friend Derek every fortnight, when I asked about Derek last week he said they had not managed to get together for a while. He used to do his own shopping every Saturday without fail. Now he asks me to pick things up."

Why this note matters. Withdrawal from activities and outings is one of the signs that compounds most quickly. Isolation affects mood, cognition, and physical health in ways that make everything else harder. Naming it early, in writing, with specific examples -- is the first step toward addressing it before it becomes entrenched.
