1 Where you are in the Quiet Shift, an honest self-assessment Not reviewed

This course exists within a larger framework called The Quiet Shift, a three-phase journey that describes the arc most families move through when supporting an aging parent. Phase 1 is awareness: you are noticing things, documenting them, building a picture. Phase 2 is acceptance: you have a clear picture and are beginning to share it, plan around it, and bring others into the conversation. Phase 3 is preparation: you are actively managing and making decisions. Understanding honestly where you sit within this framework is not about labelling your situation. It is about recognising what kind of support and what kind of tools are most relevant to where you actually are right now -- so that what you do next is the right next step, not just any next step.

How to locate yourself honestly

Phase 1 -- you are still building your picture. Things have been noticed but not yet fully named or shared. You are in the right place doing the right work. Phase 2 -- you have a clear picture and are beginning to think about conversations, family involvement, and what needs to be put in place. Phase 3 -- you are past awareness and into active management. Daily coordination, health oversight, financial or legal decisions are already part of your life. You may have started this course at Phase 1 but find yourself already operating in Phase 3 territory. That is more common than you might expect.

How to apply this to your situation

Read the three phase descriptions above and write down honestly where you are. You may feel you are between phases, or moving from one into another. That is fine, write that too. The point is not to place yourself precisely. The point is to give yourself an honest starting point for deciding what comes next. If you find that what you are dealing with is already Phase 3 territory, the tools in Modules 3 and 4 of this course -- and the tools in Course 3 -- will be more immediately relevant to your situation.

2 Who else needs to know what you have been seeing Not reviewed

What you have documented across Modules 1 and 2 is not yours to carry alone indefinitely. At some point, and the right point is different for every family, the picture you have built needs to be shared. This signal asks you to think honestly about two groups: the people in your family or close circle who need to know what you have been seeing, and the professionals -- a GP, a specialist, a social worker -- who could help interpret or act on it. Identifying these people now, before any conversation has to happen urgently, gives you time to think about how to approach them thoughtfully rather than reactively.

Who belongs in each group

Family and close circle: siblings, a partner, close friends of your parent who have also been noticing things, trusted neighbours. You do not need to tell everyone. You need to tell the people whose involvement will make the situation easier to manage, or whose absence will make it harder. Professionals: your parent's GP is usually the right first professional conversation. They can assess, refer, and advise. A geriatrician, a social worker, or a care coordinator may follow. You do not need to have arrived at a diagnosis to start this conversation. You need to have your documented observations ready to share.

How to think about timing

There is rarely a perfect moment for these conversations. There is only a right moment and a too-late moment. If you have safety concerns, particularly around driving, medication, or physical risk -- sooner is better. If the situation is concerning but stable, you have more time to plan. What you should not do is wait until a crisis forces the conversation in the worst possible circumstances. The notes you have made in this course are what makes a planned conversation possible.

Your notes

Example of a useful note

"My brother needs to know. He lives further away and I think he has been aware that something is different but has not asked directly and I have not pushed it. I will share my printed notes from this course with him as a starting point -- it is easier than trying to explain it in a phone call. Why this note matters. Named people and named conversations are far more likely to happen than vague intentions to "talk to someone." Writing down specifically who needs to know, and what you need them to understand, is the first step toward the conversations that matter most.
3 Your one honest next step, not all the steps, just the first one Not reviewed

The most common reason families stay stuck after recognising what is happening with an aging parent is not lack of care or lack of information. It is the overwhelming sense that there is so much to address that it is hard to know where to start. This signal asks you to set everything else aside and identify one thing -- the single most important thing to do next, given everything you have documented in this course so far. Not a list. Not a plan. One step. The right first step, taken clearly and intentionally, does more for your parent's safety and wellbeing than a perfect plan that never gets started.

How to identify the right first step

Look at the signals you identified as carrying the most weight in Section 2 of this module. Look at the people you named in Signal 2 of this section. Your first step is usually at the intersection of those two things -- the most significant concern, and the most important person to bring into the picture. If there is a safety issue, the first step involves addressing that safety issue. If the picture is concerning but not urgent, the first step is usually a conversation with the person who most needs to be aligned with what you are seeing.

How to make it concrete enough to actually do

A next step is only useful if it is specific enough to take. "Talk to my brother" is a start. "Call my brother on Saturday and share the printed notes from Modules 1 and 2 as a starting point for the conversation" is a next step you can actually take. The more specific you make it -- who, when, how -- the more likely it is to happen. Write it that way.

Example of a useful note

"My one next step is to book a doctor appointment for her within the next two weeks and to go with her. I will bring my printed notes from this course. That is the one thing. Everything else can follow from that."

Why this note matters. This is the note that turns everything you have built in this course from a record into an action. Write it carefully. Return to it. When you do take that step, come back and write what happened. That is how a record becomes a tool.
