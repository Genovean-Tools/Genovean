Signal Spotter: Changes in Their Body

Physical changes are often the most visible signals, and the most likely to be explained away as tiredness, age, or a difficult week. This section asks you to look at what you have actually seen, without the explanations.

Moving into Module 3

Modules 1 and 2 built your observational record and helped you understand what the pattern means. Module 3 moves into harder territory. Health and cognitive changes are the signals most families are most reluctant to name clearly, because naming them makes them real in a way that is difficult to undo. This module does not ask you to name a diagnosis. It asks you to record what you have observed, honestly and specifically, so that the people who can interpret and act on it have the information they need.

Before you begin this module

What you are about to work through may feel more confronting than what came before. That is expected and it is normal. The physical and cognitive signals in this module are the ones families most often notice first but articulate last, because they sit closest to the question that no one wants to ask directly.

This section is not asking you to reach a conclusion. It is asking you to observe and record. You are not diagnosing your parent. You are not making decisions about their care. You are doing something much simpler, and much more important: you are being honest about what you have seen. That honesty is what everything else, every conversation, every plan, every decision, will eventually depend on. Take your time with this module. Be specific. Do not soften what you have noticed to make it easier to write down.

Changes in Their Body
1 How they move, walk, and carry themselves has changed Not reviewed

Walking more slowly than they used to. A gait that has become shuffling or hesitant. Holding onto furniture, walls, or doorframes as they move through rooms, in a way that is new. Difficulty getting up from a chair, which takes more effort, more time, or requires pushing off with both arms in a way it never used to. Hesitation at stairs, stepping down from a kerb, or crossing uneven ground. Holding the handrail on steps they once took without thinking. These are not dramatic changes. They are incremental. They show up in the small physical moments of ordinary daily life, and they are the body's way of saying that something has changed in its confidence, its strength, its balance, or its coordination. Falls are one of the most serious health risks for older adults. The signs that a fall is becoming more likely usually show up in movement and posture long before a fall happens. If you have noticed a change in how your parent moves, you have noticed something worth recording carefully.

What to look for and where to look

Watch how they get up from a chair when they do not know you are watching. Observe how they move through the kitchen, do they use the counter for support in a way they did not used to? Notice how they walk from the car to the door, or across a car park. Look at stairs and kerbs. Watch whether they reach for your arm in situations they would previously have managed independently. The moments you are looking for are small, physical, and easy to miss if you are not paying attention to movement specifically. Start paying attention to movement.

How to observe without making them self-conscious

Do not watch them directly and do not comment on what you notice. Your parent's instinct, if they sense they are being observed, will be to perform capability they may not fully have. Watch sideways. Watch in the background. Watch in the moments between conversations, when they stand up to make tea or walk to another room. Those unguarded moments are where you will see the clearest picture. If you have already commented and they have pushed back, that pushback is itself worth noting, it tells you they are aware of the change even if they are not prepared to acknowledge it.

Your notes

Example of a useful note

"He gets up from his chair differently now. He pauses at the edge of the seat first, pushes off with both hands, and steadies himself before taking a step. He has been doing this for about four months. He also walks more slowly than he did, I noticed it clearly when we were walking from the car park to the restaurant last month. He held onto my arm without me offering it, which he has never done before. He used to walk quickly and firmly. This is not the same walk."

Why this note matters. A doctor assessing fall risk, a physiotherapist reviewing mobility, or a family member who has not been present will find specific observations about movement far more useful than a general concern. Writing what you have seen precisely, the chair, the walk, the reaching for support, turns a feeling into clinical information.
2 Their physical appearance or body has changed noticeably Not reviewed

Weight loss that makes clothes hang differently. A face that looks thinner or more drawn than you remember. Muscle mass that has visibly reduced, arms that look smaller, legs that seem less substantial. Skin that has changed in colour, texture, or condition in ways that feel new. An overall sense that the person in front of you looks physically different from the person you knew six or twelve months ago. These changes accumulate gradually and are particularly easy to miss when you see someone regularly, the comparison point moves with you. The clearest signal is often the reaction you have when you see them after a gap, or when you look at a recent photograph alongside one from a year ago and notice a difference you cannot attribute to the photograph. Unintentional weight loss in older adults is medically significant and warrants a doctor conversation sooner rather than later. Do not let the normalisation of gradual change stop you from recording what you are actually seeing.

What to look for and where to look

Look at how their clothes fit. A belt that is fastened on a tighter notch, trousers that bag at the waist, a shirt that was once fitted now hanging loose, these are concrete, specific indicators of weight change that are often clearer than asking directly. Look at their face and hands. Look at how they sit -- is there less physical presence to them than there used to be? If you visit regularly, try to hold a fixed comparison point in your mind from six months ago and compare deliberately, rather than accepting what you currently see as the new normal.

How to raise this if you need to

If you are concerned about weight or physical appearance, do not lead with the observation directly. "How has your appetite been lately?" or "Are you managing to eat well?" opens the conversation without making it about how they look. Their answer will often tell you more than the question does. If they dismiss the question quickly or change the subject, note that. Defensive minimising of a question about eating is itself a signal worth recording.

Your notes

Example of a useful note

"She looks lighter than she did in the summer. Her trousers are too big now I, noticed because she was wearing a belt on its last hole and the fabric was still bunching at the waist. Her face looks more drawn. I looked at a photo on her mantelpiece from Christmas and compared it to her sitting across from me and the difference was visible enough that I had to stop myself from saying something. I don't know how much weight she has lost but it is noticeable."

Why this note matters. Unintentional weight loss is one of the signals that GPs take most seriously. A note that describes specifically what you have seen, the clothing, the comparison to a photograph, the timeframe -- gives a doctor something concrete to work with in an appointment. Do not wait until the change is dramatic to record it.
3 Small physical signs in everyday moments that were not there before Not reviewed

This signal is about the small physical things, the ones that do not individually look like much but that together, or in the context of what else you have noticed, tell a story. Hands that tremble slightly when holding a cup. A grip that has become noticeably weaker when you take their hand. Difficulty with buttons, zips, or small fastenings that they used to manage easily. Fumbling with keys or a phone in a way that is new. Dropping things more than before. Spilling food or drink in a way that is out of character. Difficulty cutting food, opening jars, or managing tasks that require fine motor control. Fatigue that sets in more quickly than it used to -- sitting down after a short walk, becoming visibly tired by mid-afternoon, losing energy in situations that would not previously have required effort. These are the signals that happen in the small, unnoticed moments of daily life. Individually, they are easy to attribute to tiredness or a difficult day. As a pattern across multiple visits, they are telling you something about physical capacity that is worth documenting carefully.

What to look for in everyday moments

Notice how they handle objects, a cup, a key, a pen. Watch how they manage with food at a meal. Pay attention to fatigue, when does it show up, how quickly does it arrive, how visible is it? Look at their hands. Notice if they take longer to do things that used to be automatic. The moments this signal appears in are the moments between conversations and activities, when the body is just doing what it does. Start noticing those moments specifically.

How to record small things without dismissing them

Small physical changes are the easiest to dismiss as irrelevant and the hardest to remember accurately later. Write them down on the day you notice them if you can, even in a note on your phone. The specific detail -- what they were doing, what you saw, when it was -- is what gives these observations their value. "I think his hands tremble sometimes" is less useful than "I noticed his hands shaking when he was pouring tea on Tuesday. He spilled some and looked briefly embarrassed before continuing."

Your notes

Example of a useful note

"His grip is noticeably weaker. I noticed it when we shook hands at Christmas, his handshake was loose in a way that was unusual. He has also been dropping things more. In the last month I have seen him drop his phone twice, drop a cup once, and struggle with the zip on his jacket before giving up and leaving it undone. He seems to tire more quickly too -- by mid-afternoon on our last visit he was clearly flagging in a way he would not have been two years ago."

Why this note matters. Fine motor changes, tremor, and rapid fatigue can each point toward specific conditions that are far more manageable when identified early. A list of specific, dated observations -- even small ones -- is exactly what helps a doctor move from general concern to targeted assessment. Your everyday observations are clinically valuable. Write them as if they are.
